Collaboration & Communication Mini-Lessons

These lessons build the interpersonal, presentational, and professional communication skills students need to thrive in collaborative passion projects.

Lesson 1: Kind Feedback: Stars and Wishes

⏱  Duration: 10 min   🎓  Grade Band: PreK–2

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will practice giving specific, kind peer feedback using the Stars and Wishes framework.

📚 Standards

  • CCSS.ELA.SL.K.1 — Participate in collaborative conversations
  • CCSS.ELA.SL.1.1b — Build on others' talk in conversations

📋 Materials

  • Star and wish anchor chart
  • Student work samples (drawings or projects)
  • Sentence stem cards

🚀 Launch

SAY: “When we see someone’s work, we want to help them make it BETTER — and we want to help them feel PROUD. We can do both at the same time.”

SHOW a student drawing (with permission) or a teacher-made example.

ASK: “If this were YOUR work, what would feel good to hear? What would help you make it even better?”

📣 Teach It

TEACH the Stars and Wishes framework:

STAR: Something specific you genuinely liked. NOT just 'good job.'

Example star: 'I like how you used so many colors' OR 'Your animal has a lot of detail — I can tell it’s a tiger'

WISH: Something kind and specific that could make it better.

Example wish: 'I wish I could see what’s in the background' OR 'I wonder what sounds your animal makes — could you add that?'

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Show a sample drawing. Give one star: 'I notice you drew the whole family — that tells me this is really important to you.'

Give one wish: 'I wish I knew where they are. Could you add a background?'

SAY: “Notice: I was specific. I wasn’t mean. And my wish was more like a question than a command.”

📝 Practice

Partners share a piece of work from their passion project. Each gives one star and one wish using sentence stems from the card. Listen without interrupting. Say 'thank you' after receiving feedback — not 'but...'

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Feedback stems on printed cards

•  Practice giving feedback on a teacher-made example first

•  Focus on only the star (positive) for first round

Extensions

•  Write feedback instead of just saying it

•  Give 2 stars and 2 wishes

•  Apply feedback received and explain what changed in their work

🤖 AI Connection

  • Teacher models using AI to get feedback on a drawing or piece of writing.
  • Show AI’s response. ASK: “Did AI give a specific star? A helpful wish? Was it kind?”
  • Discuss: “Could AI give better feedback if we told it to use the Stars and Wishes format?” Try it.

🚪 Exit Ticket

Tell your partner: one thing you’re going to change based on their wish, and why.

Lesson 2: Presentation Voice: Volume, Pace, and Eye Contact

⏱  Duration: 15 min   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 3–5

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will identify and practice the three most impactful verbal presentation skills and deliver a 60-second practice share with structured feedback.

📚 Standards

  • CCSS.ELA.SL.4.4 — Report on a topic or text using appropriate facts and relevant details, speaking clearly

📋 Materials

  • Presentation skills anchor chart
  • 60-second timer
  • Peer feedback form (volume / pace / eye contact checkboxes)

🚀 Launch

SAY: “Have you ever listened to someone present and realized partway through that you have no idea what they said? What went wrong?”

Take responses. SAY: “Content alone isn’t enough. How you deliver it determines whether anyone receives it.”

📣 Teach It

TEACH 3 presentation skills:

VOLUME: Speak so the person farthest away can hear you clearly. Project — don’t shout.

PACE: Slow down more than feels comfortable. You know what you’re going to say. They don’t.

EYE CONTACT: Look at individuals for 2–3 seconds each. Not at the ceiling. Not at your paper. At people.

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Deliver the same 30-second content 3 times: too quiet, too fast, poor eye contact; then with one skill improved; then with all 3.

Class identifies which version was most effective and names exactly which skills made the difference.

📝 Practice

Students prepare a 60-second share about their passion project (topic, one interesting fact, why they care). Deliver to a group of 3. Each listener fills out the feedback form: which skill was strongest? Which needs practice? Debrief together.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  30 seconds instead of 60

•  Bullet-point notes allowed on an index card

•  Practice volume and pace only (2 skills instead of 3)

Extensions

•  Add a 4th skill: gestures (no hands in pockets, no fidgeting)

•  Video-record their practice share and self-evaluate

•  Coach a partner on their weakest skill using specific language

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students ask AI to evaluate a speech transcript for clarity and engagement.
  • Discuss: 'AI can read the words. But what can’t AI evaluate about a presentation?'
  • Answer together: volume, pace, eye contact, emotion, presence.

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students write their strongest skill and one thing they’ll practice before their actual presentation.

Lesson 3: Critique Protocol: Giving and Receiving Honest Feedback

⏱  Duration: 20 min   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 6–8

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will apply a structured critique protocol to give and receive specific, evidence-based feedback on passion project work in progress.

📚 Standards

  • CCSS.ELA.SL.6.1 — Engage effectively in collaborative discussions
  • CCSS.ELA.SL.7.1d — Acknowledge new information expressed by others

📋 Materials

  • Critique protocol posted
  • Work in progress (draft, prototype, or outline)
  • Feedback tracking sheet (for presenter)

🚀 Launch

SAY: “The most dangerous words in peer feedback are 'It’s great!' and 'I like it.' Both tell the creator nothing useful.”

SAY: “We’re going to practice something uncomfortable and useful: honest, specific, evidence-based critique.'”

📣 Teach It

TEACH the 4-step critique protocol:

Step 1 — DESCRIBE: What do you observe? (No evaluation yet.) 'I notice that the first section focuses on... the middle shifts to... the ending...'

Step 2 — WONDER: Ask an honest question. 'I’m curious why you chose to... What would happen if...?'

Step 3 — STRENGTHEN: Offer one specific suggestion. 'The argument might be stronger if...'

Step 4 — AFFIRM: Name one thing that is genuinely working. Be specific. ('The opening question is grabbing because...')

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Apply the protocol to a teacher-created draft project excerpt.

Step 1: “I notice the introduction gives a lot of context but holds the thesis until paragraph 3.”

Step 2: “I’m curious whether starting with the thesis would create more focus earlier.”

Step 3: “The argument might be stronger if the statistics were introduced before the anecdote.”

Step 4: “The specific example in paragraph 2 is the most compelling moment in the piece.”

📝 Practice

Pairs share one piece of project work. Listener applies all 4 protocol steps. Presenter listens without responding until the listener is done, then asks one clarifying question. Switch roles. Each person records the most useful feedback they received.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Use Steps 2 and 4 only (wonder + affirm)

•  Critique sentence starters for each step on a card

•  Practice on a teacher-provided sample before personal work

Extensions

•  Write a written critique using the protocol instead of verbal

•  Identify 3 pieces of feedback they received and evaluate which is most actionable

•  Design a feedback protocol adapted for a specific creative form (film, visual art, coding)

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students ask AI to critique their work draft: 'Critique this [project element] using 4 steps: describe what you observe, ask a question, suggest one improvement, identify what’s working.'
  • Compare AI’s critique to the partner’s critique.
  • Discuss: 'Which critique was more useful? Which was more honest? Which one knew the creator’s intention?'

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students write the one piece of feedback they received that they will act on first, and explain why they chose it.

Lesson 4: Professional Communication: Emails, Outreach, and Follow-Up

⏱  Duration: 20 min   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 9–10

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will write and evaluate professional outreach emails to community members, experts, or mentors for their passion projects.

📚 Standards

  • CCSS.ELA.W.9-10.4 — Produce clear and coherent writing appropriate to task, purpose, and audience

📋 Materials

  • Sample outreach emails (strong and weak examples)
  • Email drafting template
  • Peer review checklist

🚀 Launch

SAY: “You can do extraordinary research and have a brilliant idea. But if you can’t communicate professionally with the people who can help you, none of it moves forward.”

SAY: “An email to a community expert or potential mentor is your first impression. You have 30 seconds.”

📣 Teach It

TEACH the 5-component professional email:

1. SUBJECT LINE: Specific, clear, and why they should open it

2. OPENING: Who you are and why you’re contacting them (not 'Hi, my name is...')

3. CONTEXT: What you’re working on and why it connects to them

4. CLEAR ASK: One specific, reasonable request (not 'Can you help me?')

5. PROFESSIONAL CLOSE: Thank them for their time, offer flexibility, sign off with full name and school

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Show a weak email ('Hi, I’m working on a project and need help.'). Identify what’s missing.

Rewrite it live using the 5 components. Show how specificity and professionalism change the response rate.

SAY: “People respond to emails that respect their time and show the writer knows something about them.”

📝 Practice

Students draft a real outreach email to an actual expert or community member connected to their project. Apply the 5-component template. Partner peer review using the checklist: all 5 present? Subject line compelling? Ask clear and specific? Professional close? Revise before sending (or submitting as draft).

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Complete subject line and opening only; teacher supports rest

•  Choose from a list of 5 potential recipient types

•  Email template with sentence starters for each component

Extensions

•  Write 3 variations of the same email for different recipients

•  Draft a follow-up email for if they don’t receive a response in 2 weeks

•  Research proper email etiquette differences across professional cultures

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students ask AI: 'Draft a professional outreach email from a high school student to [type of expert] about [their project topic].'
  • Evaluate AI’s draft: Is it too generic? Too formal? Does it sound like them?
  • Revise to make it authentically theirs — with AI as a starting draft, not a final product.

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students share the one revision they made to their email that most improved it, and explain the principle behind the change.

Lesson 5: The Formal Presentation: Structure, Presence, and Handling Tough Questions

⏱  Duration: 30 min   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 11–12

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will practice delivering a structured formal presentation with professional presence and responding to challenging questions using the EPIC framework.

📚 Standards

  • CCSS.ELA.SL.11-12.4 — Present information clearly, concisely, and logically
  • CCSS.ELA.SL.11-12.6 — Adapt speech to a variety of contexts

📋 Materials

  • Presentation structure template
  • EPIC response framework card
  • Peer evaluation form
  • Timer

🚀 Launch

SAY: “When experts present at a conference, they’re not winging it. When a startup pitches investors, every sentence is intentional. When a scientist defends a dissertation, they’ve prepared for every question.”

SAY: “Your capstone defense is your version of that. Let’s make sure you’re ready.”

📣 Teach It

TEACH 5-part presentation structure:

1. HOOK (30–60 sec): A question, statistic, story, or provocation that earns attention immediately

2. CONTEXT (60–90 sec): Why this topic matters and what gap your project addresses

3. CORE WORK (5–8 min): Your methodology, key findings, and product demonstration

4. IMPLICATIONS (60–90 sec): What your findings mean and who should care

5. CALL TO ACTION / CLOSE (30–60 sec): What you want the audience to do, think, or feel differently

TEACH presence principles:

Pause before beginning — silence commands attention

Slow down — expertise sounds unhurried

Plant your feet — movement should be purposeful, not nervous

Welcome questions — confidence is shown by how you handle what you didn’t expect

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Teacher (or invited guest) delivers the first 2 minutes of a presentation using the structure.

Class evaluates: Which part was the hook? Where was the context? Was presence confident?

Then ask the presenter a tough question. Model the EPIC response framework live.

📝 Practice

Students deliver a 4–6 minute presentation of their capstone. Audience members complete peer evaluation forms. One panel member asks 2 challenging questions; presenter uses EPIC. Full group debrief: what was most effective? What needs work before the real defense?

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  4 minutes instead of 6

•  Use notes on index cards

•  Deliver only sections 1–3; teacher fills in 4 and 5 as example

Extensions

•  Film and self-evaluate against the rubric

•  Give the same presentation to a different audience (a younger class, a community member) and adapt the content

•  Design and conduct a full mock capstone defense with 3 classmates as panelists

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students ask AI to evaluate their presentation outline: 'Does my presentation structure include all 5 components? Is the hook strong enough to earn attention in the first 30 seconds?'
  • Then: 'Generate 5 tough questions a panel might ask about this [topic/argument].'
  • Students practice EPIC responses to AI-generated questions before the live practice.

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students write: their strongest presentation moment in today’s practice, and the one skill they’ll focus on most before the real defense.
