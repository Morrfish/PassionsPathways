Lesson 1: Where Does Information Come From?

⏱  Duration: 10 min   🎓  Grade Band: PreK–2

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will identify 4 places where information can be found and match types of questions to the best source for answering them.

📚 Standards

  • CCSS.ELA.RI.K.5 — Identify the front cover and back cover of a book
  • CCSS.ELA.RI.1.5 — Know and use various text features

📋 Materials

  • Source type cards (book, video, person, website)
  • Question cards
  • Sorting mat

🚀 Launch

SAY: “Imagine you want to know how to make the perfect grilled cheese. Where would you look?”

Take responses. SAY: “There’s more than one right answer. And knowing WHERE to look is just as important as finding the answer.”

📣 Teach It

TEACH 4 source types:

Book: great for deep, detailed information

Video: great for seeing something in action or explained step by step

Person/expert: great for personal experience and things not written anywhere

Website: great for current and quick information (but check who made it!)

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Hold up a question card: 'How do caterpillars turn into butterflies?'

Think aloud: “Could I look at a book? Yes. Watch a video? Maybe even better — I could SEE it happen. Ask a scientist? Perfect. Website? Yes. All could work — for DIFFERENT reasons.”

📝 Practice

Students receive 4 question cards. Sort each onto the source type that would work BEST. Compare with a partner. Discuss any disagreements.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Match source type to only 2 questions

•  Picture icons on each source type card

•  Pre-sorted first example with class together

Extensions

•  Create a new question card that would need 2 different source types

•  Write a sentence explaining why they chose the source for each question

•  Find one real example of each source type in the classroom library

🤖 AI Connection

  • Teacher demonstration: 'Let’s ask our AI helper this question. What kind of source IS AI? Is it a book? A person? A website?'
  • Discuss: AI learned from all those source types. Does that make it better or just different?

🚪 Exit Ticket

Point to one question card. Tell a partner which source you’d use and why.

Lesson 2: Notes in Your Own Words: No Copying Allowed

⏱  Duration: 15 min   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 3–5

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will distinguish between copying and note-taking, and practice transforming source information into their own words using a 3-step process.

📚 Standards

  • CCSS.ELA.W.4.8 — Recall relevant information from experiences or gather information from print and digital sources
  • CCSS.ELA.W.5.8 — Gather relevant information from multiple print and digital sources

📋 Materials

  • Short informational paragraph printed for each student
  • Blank note-taking box
  • 'In my own words' sentence starters

🚀 Launch

SAY: “If I take a sentence from a book and copy it onto my notes, I now know the words. But do I actually know what they MEAN?”

SAY: “Real note-taking is a translation: you take what the text says and put it into words that show YOUR brain understood it.”

📣 Teach It

TEACH 3-step own-words note-taking:

Step 1: Read the paragraph. Put it face-down.

Step 2: Ask yourself: 'What did that say? What’s the main point?'

Step 3: Write only what you remember — in your own words — without peeking.

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Read a paragraph aloud. Turn it over. Think aloud: “The main idea was... it also said... I think it means...” Write notes.

Compare to original: 'Did I change the wording? Did I keep the meaning? That’s the goal.'

📝 Practice

Students read an assigned paragraph. Turn it face-down. Write 3 notes from memory. Flip and compare. Circle anything they copied accidentally. Rewrite those in their own words.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Sentence starters: 'This paragraph is about... One important thing is...'

•  Read only first 2 sentences, take notes from those

•  Partner reads aloud while other takes notes (no visual of text)

Extensions

•  Take notes from 2 paragraphs and synthesize them into 4 bullets

•  Develop a personal note-taking system with symbols and abbreviations

•  Compare their notes to a partner’s: what did each person include or leave out?

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students ask AI to summarize the same paragraph. Compare: Is AI’s summary in 'AI’s own words'?
  • Discuss: “If AI can summarize but doesn’t understand, what does that tell us about the difference between notes and understanding?”

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students write 3 notes on their topic in their own words, without looking at any source. Share with a partner.

Lesson 3: Building an Annotated Bibliography

⏱  Duration: 20 min   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 6–8

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will create an annotated bibliography entry for each of their passion project sources, including citation, summary, evaluation, and relevance notes.

📚 Standards

  • CCSS.ELA.W.6.8 — Gather relevant information from multiple print and digital sources
  • CCSS.ELA.W.8.8 — Assess the credibility and accuracy of each source

📋 Materials

  • 3 sources per student (from research in progress)
  • Annotated bibliography template
  • Citation format guide (MLA or APA)

🚀 Launch

SAY: “A bibliography just lists what you used. An ANNOTATED bibliography shows you THOUGHT about what you used.”

SAY: “When you’re 3 weeks into a project and you can’t remember why you added a source, your annotation saves you. When a teacher wants to know you actually read your sources, your annotation proves it.”

📣 Teach It

TEACH 4 parts of an annotation:

1. CITATION: Full citation in MLA or APA format

2. SUMMARY: 2–3 sentences: What does this source argue or explain?

3. EVALUATION: 1–2 sentences: Is it credible? Why? Any bias or limitations?

4. RELEVANCE: 1 sentence: How will you use this in your project?

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Write a full annotation for one source on the projector, thinking aloud at each step.

SAY: “The relevance note is the most important. It’s me promising myself: this is why this source earns its place.”

📝 Practice

Students write 3 complete annotations using their own project sources, one for each of the 4 required components. Partner review: Does each annotation fully cover all 4 parts? Is the relevance note specific to their project?

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Complete 1 annotation instead of 3

•  Pre-format the citation; student writes only summary, evaluation, relevance

•  Sentence stems provided for each section

Extensions

•  Add a 5th component: 'How does this source connect to or conflict with Source X?'

•  Write annotations for 5–6 sources

•  Rank all sources by usefulness to the project and explain the ranking

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students use AI to help format one citation: 'Format this source in MLA format [paste source info].'
  • Verify: Does the AI format match the citation guide? Correct any errors.
  • Discuss: 'AI can format citations, but can it evaluate whether a source is credible for YOUR specific project?'

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students identify the source they will use MOST in their project and write a one-sentence explanation of exactly how it will be used.

Lesson 4: Working with Data: Reading, Interpreting, and Using Statistics

⏱  Duration: 25 min   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 9–10

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will apply a 4-step data literacy framework to interpret statistical evidence and integrate data-based claims appropriately into their passion projects.

📚 Standards

  • CCSS.ELA.RI.9-10.7 — Analyze various accounts of a subject told in different mediums

📋 Materials

  • 3–4 data sets or charts related to a class topic
  • Data interpretation guide
  • Project evidence log

🚀 Launch

SAY: “73% of statistics are made up on the spot. (Pause.) I made that one up. But you almost believed it for a second.”

SAY: “Numbers feel authoritative. But they can mislead, misrepresent, or simply be wrong. Learning to read statistics critically is one of the most useful things you’ll do this year.”

📣 Teach It

TEACH 4-step data literacy framework:

Step 1: SOURCE — Who collected this data? Who funded it? What is their interest?

Step 2: SAMPLE — Who was included? Who was left out? How many people? How were they selected?

Step 3: MEANING — What does this number actually measure? Is that the same as what you need?

Step 4: CORRELATION vs. CAUSATION — Does this data prove something caused something else, or just that two things happened at the same time?

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Show a chart. Apply each step aloud.

Step 1: Source is a marketing company. Red flag.

Step 2: Sample was 200 online respondents. Self-selected. Not representative.

Step 3: They measured 'satisfaction' — but satisfaction and effectiveness aren’t the same.

Step 4: Sales went up after the product launched, but so did the economy. Correlation, not causation.

📝 Practice

Students apply the 4-step framework to 2 data sets from their project research. Write a 1-paragraph data claim: 'According to [source], [statistic]. This is [reliable/limited] because [framework finding], and I will use it to support my argument that [connection to thesis].'

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Apply only Steps 1 and 2 instead of all 4

•  Pre-analyzed data set 1; students analyze data set 2

•  Data analysis graphic organizer with labeled boxes

Extensions

•  Find and evaluate 3 conflicting statistics on the same topic

•  Calculate margin of error or significance level for their own survey data

•  Write a paragraph explaining why two reputable sources report different statistics on the same issue

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students ask AI: 'What are the limitations of the following statistic: [paste statistic and source]?'
  • Compare AI’s critique to their own 4-step analysis.
  • Discuss: 'Can AI evaluate whether data is relevant to YOUR specific thesis? What does that require that AI doesn’t have?'

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students write one data claim sentence using a statistic from their project, including its source and one limitation.

Lesson 5: The Annotated Methodology: Documenting How You Researched

⏱  Duration: 25 min   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 11–12

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will write a research methodology section for their capstone that transparently documents their research design, source selection criteria, and data collection process.

📚 Standards

  • CCSS.ELA.W.11-12.7 — Conduct sustained research projects
  • CCSS.ELA.W.11-12.8 — Gather relevant information from multiple authoritative sources

📋 Materials

  • Research methodology template
  • Students' annotated bibliographies
  • Source selection rationale worksheet

🚀 Launch

SAY: “In any serious research, the methodology is as important as the findings. Why? Because a reader who can’t see HOW you found your evidence can’t evaluate whether to trust what you found.”

SAY: “Your capstone methodology section answers: What did you look for? Where did you look? How did you decide what counted as evidence? And what are the limits of your approach?”

📣 Teach It

TEACH 5 methodology components:

1. RESEARCH DESIGN: What type of research did you conduct? (Literature review, primary research, mixed methods?)

2. SOURCE CRITERIA: What made a source qualify for inclusion? (Peer-reviewed? Published after [year]? Primary source?)

3. SEARCH PROCESS: How did you find sources? (Which databases, keywords, date ranges?)

4. DATA COLLECTION: How did you gather primary data? (Survey design, interview protocol, observation criteria?)

5. LIMITATIONS: What couldn’t you access? What biases might your process have introduced?

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Write the first 3 sentences of a methodology section for a class-familiar capstone topic.

SAY: “Notice: I don’t write 'I searched Google.' I write 'I conducted a systematic search of [database] using the search terms [X], [Y], [Z], filtered to peer-reviewed publications from 2015–2024.'”

SAY: “Specificity is transparency. Transparency is intellectual honesty.”

📝 Practice

Students draft their own methodology section addressing all 5 components. Partner review: Are all 5 components present? Is the source criteria specific? Is the limitations section honest about gaps? Revise before submission.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Focus on components 1, 2, and 5 only

•  Sentence frames for each component

•  Use a class-provided research scenario instead of their own

Extensions

•  Add a section on how AI was integrated into the research process and how its outputs were verified

•  Compare methodology to a published research article in their field

•  Write a reflective paragraph: how would you design the methodology differently if you had more time/resources?

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students ask AI: 'Based on my capstone topic [describe], what are 3 potential limitations or biases in a literature review on this topic?'
  • Integrate relevant limitations into their methodology section.
  • Discuss: 'If AI identifies limitations in your research design, does that change the research, or just the methodology section?'

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students identify the most significant limitation of their research design and write 2 sentences explaining how it affects how their findings should be interpreted.

Project 1: My Favorite Thing in the World

⏱  Time Frame: 1–2 weeks

❓  Driving Question: What do I love, and how can I show others why it is so special to me?

🎯 Learning Goals

  • Identify and name a personal interest or favorite topic
  • Create a simple visual product (drawing, model, or slideshow) that communicates ideas
  • Use descriptive words to explain their work to a peer or the class
  • Practice basic reflection using sentence stems

📚 Standards Alignment

ELA / Literacy

•  CCSS.ELA.SL.K.4 – Describe familiar people, places, things, events

•  CCSS.ELA.SL.1.5 – Add drawings or other visual displays to descriptions

•  CCSS.ELA.W.2.3 – Narrative writing with details

21st Century / SEL

•  Curiosity & Imagination

•  Communication

•  Creative Expression

🏫 Scripted Mini-Lessons

Mini-Lesson 1: How to Choose Your Topic (Day 1, 10 minutes)

•  SAY: “Todayyou get to be the expert. An expert is someone who knows a lot about something they love.”

•  SHOW: Display the picture choice menu. Point to each category.

•  SAY: “Look at each picture. Which one makes your heart feel happy or excited?”

•  DO: Students point to or circle their choice on the menu.

•  DEBRIEF: “Notice how everyone picked something different? That’s what makes you YOU.”

Mini-Lesson 2: Adding Details to a Drawing (Day 2, 8 minutes)

•  SAY: “A great drawing tells a story without words. Details help people understand what you mean.”

•  ASK: “Which drawing tells me MORE about cats?”

•  DO: Students practice adding 2 details to their first sketch.

•  PROMPT: “What is ONE thing about your topic that most people don’t know? Draw it!”

  • Simple 3-slide presentation: My topic / Why I love it / One cool fact (Grade 2)
  • LEGO or block model with a verbal explanation
  • Show-and-tell video (60 seconds, teacher-recorded)

📋 Checkpoints

  • Checkpoint 1 — Day 1: Topic chosen (teacher records on class tracker)
  • Checkpoint 2 — Day 2: Drawing started with at least 1 detail
  • Checkpoint 3 — Day 4: Product mostly complete (teacher gives verbal feedback)
  • Checkpoint 4 — Day 5: Student shares with at least one peer

🏫 Scripted Mini-Lesson: How Might We (HMW) Problem Framing

Day 2 — 20 minutes

•  SAY: “Designers don’t just say ‘that’s a problem.’ They turn problems into opportunities using a special sentence: How Might We...”

•  EXAMPLE: Problem: “The cafeteria is too loud.”

•     Too narrow HMW: “How might we make the cafeteria quieter?” (limits solutions)

•     Too broad HMW: “How might we fix all communication at school?” (too vague)

•     Just right: “How might we create a calmer lunchtime experience for all students?”

🏫 Scripted Mini-Lesson: Writing a Thesis-Driven Capstone Proposal

Week 1 — 30 minutes (full workshop)

•  SAY: “The proposal is not just a plan — it’s a promise. It tells your panel: I know exactly what I’m doing, why it matters, and how I’ll know if I succeeded.”

•  TEACH the 5 elements of a strong capstone proposal:

•     1. PASSION CONNECTION: Why does this topic matter to YOU personally?

•     2. PROBLEM/THESIS: What are you arguing, solving, or building — and why does it matter?

•     3. PRODUCT: What will exist at the end that didn’t exist before?

•     4. EVIDENCE PLAN: What research, data, or expertise will guide your work?

•     5. ETHICS/AI PLAN: What ethical considerations apply? How will AI be used responsibly?

•  WORKSHOP: Students draft a one-paragraph version of each section.

•  PEER REVIEW: Partners use a proposal checklist to identify gaps or vague claims.

•  REVISE: Students refine before formal proposal submission.

•  DEBRIEF: “A proposal that a stranger can understand — without context — is a strong proposal.”
