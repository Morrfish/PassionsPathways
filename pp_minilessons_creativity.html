Creativity & Innovation Mini-Lessons

Lesson 1: Brainstorming Bonanza: Lots of Ideas Fast

⏱  Duration: 10 min   🎓  Grade Band: PreK–2

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students generate as many ideas as possible on a topic in a timed brainstorm, building the habit of quantity-first creative thinking.

📚 Standards

  • CCSS.ELA.SL.K.6 — Speak audibly and express thoughts and feelings clearly

📋 Materials

  • Large paper per table
  • Markers (lots of colors)
  • Sand timer (2 min)
  • Topic cards

🚀 Launch

SAY: “Creativity rule #1: the FIRST idea is almost never the BEST idea. Great creators keep going until they have SO many ideas that the good ones start to appear.”

SAY: “We’re going to do a brainstorm race. Ready? GO FAST. NO judging. ALL ideas count.”

📣 Teach It

TEACH: 3 brainstorming rules:

1. No bad ideas — every idea goes down

2. Build on each other's ideas

3. Keep going even when you feel stuck — that’s when the best ideas appear

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Timer 60 seconds. Teacher writes silly, creative, and obvious ideas about 'What could you do with a cardboard box?' without pausing.

COUNT the ideas: “I got 14! Let’s see if you can beat me.”

📝 Practice

Teams brainstorm 'All the things we could create about [theme].' Set timer for 2 minutes. Count ideas. Celebrate the table with the most. Then circle the 3 most interesting ones.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Draw ideas instead of writing

•  Pre-printed idea starters to spark thinking

•  Work with teacher at a small table

Extensions

•  Take one circled idea and sketch a full plan for it

•  Sort ideas into categories

•  Present the most creative idea to another table

🤖 AI Connection

  • Teacher shows AI brainstorming 10 ideas in 10 seconds
  • SAY: “AI is fast at brainstorming. But look — did it think of anything only YOU could think of?”

🚪 Exit Ticket

Point to your favorite idea from the brainstorm. Tell a partner why.

Lesson 2: The Remix: Make Something Old Into Something New

⏱  Duration: 15 min   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 3–5

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will apply creative remixing — taking an existing idea and transforming it with a new angle, format, or audience.

📚 Standards

  • CCSS.ELA.W.4.3 — Write narratives to develop real or imagined experiences
  • CCSS.ELA.W.5.5 — With guidance, develop and strengthen writing

📋 Materials

  • Remix prompt cards
  • Examples of famous remixes (Romeo & Juliet → West Side Story, etc.)
  • Blank planning sheet

🚀 Launch

SAY: “Almost every 'new' creative idea is actually a REMIX of something that came before. West Side Story is Romeo and Juliet in New York. Hamilton is American history as hip-hop. The key is: what new angle, audience, or format makes it YOUR version?”

SHOW 2–3 famous remix examples and ask: 'What did the creator change? What did they keep?'

📣 Teach It

TEACH 4 remix strategies:

1. Change the AUDIENCE: same story, different age group or culture

2. Change the FORMAT: book → podcast, speech → visual, recipe → song

3. Change the ANGLE: tell the villain's side, the bystander's view

4. Change the SETTING: same plot, different time/place

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Take a fairy tale (e.g., Cinderella). Apply remix strategy 3: Tell the story from the stepmother's perspective.

SAY: “I didn’t invent the story. But the angle is entirely mine.”

📝 Practice

Students choose a topic from their passion project and apply one remix strategy: What's the 'old' version? What strategy did you choose? What does your remix look like? Sketch or outline the remix idea.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Choose from a list of 5 existing stories to remix

•  Remix strategy cards with examples

•  Partner remix instead of solo

Extensions

•  Apply two remix strategies at once

•  Pitch the remix to the class: sell why YOUR angle is the most interesting

•  Create the actual remixed product as a side project

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students ask AI to suggest 3 remix ideas for their topic: “Here is my passion project topic [X]. Suggest 3 creative remix angles I could take.”
  • Evaluate: Which is most original? Which feels most like them?
  • SAY: “Can AI remix? What’s the difference between AI remixing and YOU remixing?”

🚪 Exit Ticket

Name the remix strategy you used and explain in one sentence what makes your version different from the original.

Lesson 3: Prototyping Fast: Make It Before You’re Ready

⏱  Duration: 20 min   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 6–8

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will create a low-fidelity prototype of their passion project idea in under 15 minutes, developing comfort with imperfection and rapid iteration.

📚 Standards

  • NGSS ETS1-B — Developing Possible Solutions
  • CCSS.ELA.SL.7.5 — Include multimedia components in presentations

📋 Materials

  • Mixed materials: index cards, sticky notes, paper, markers, pipe cleaners, tape
  • Timer
  • Critique protocol sheet

🚀 Launch

SAY: “Designers have a motto: 'Build to think, not think to build.' The fastest way to find out if an idea works is to make a rough version of it immediately — even if it’s messy, unfinished, and held together with tape.”

SAY: “Today you have 12 minutes to prototype something about your project. Go.”

📣 Teach It

TEACH before the timer: 3 prototyping principles:

1. Rough is fine — it’s meant to communicate an idea, not impress anyone

2. Label everything you can’t build yet (write 'VIDEO CLIP HERE' on a card)

3. A prototype answers a question. What question is yours answering?

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Teacher takes a random topic (e.g., 'app to help students track homework') and builds a paper prototype in 5 minutes in front of the class.

SAY: “See this sticky note? That’s the log-in screen. This card? The notification system. I can test the flow without writing a single line of code.”

📝 Practice

Students prototype for 12 minutes (minimal materials). Then structured critique: 2 minutes to explain what the prototype is and what question it answers. Partner gives 2 positive observations and 1 question it raises. Students write 1 revision they want to make.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Pre-defined what to prototype (one specific element of the project)

•  Provide a template: 'My prototype shows ___ and answers the question ___'

•  Label-only prototype allowed (no building required)

Extensions

•  Build a version 2 in a second 12-minute sprint

•  Document the evolution of version 1 to version 2 in a design journal

•  Conduct a user test with someone outside the class and report findings

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students ask AI: “Here is my project idea [describe]. What is the one most important thing a prototype of this should test?”
  • Use AI’s suggestion to set a clearer purpose for the next prototype sprint.
  • Discuss: “Could AI prototype? What can only a human prototype communicate?”

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students write: What question did your prototype answer? What new question did it create?

Lesson 4: Constraints as Creativity: Why Limits Make Better Work

⏱  Duration: 20 min   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 9–10

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will explore how creative constraints — limits on time, format, or resources — drive innovation, and apply a self-imposed constraint to their project work.

📚 Standards

  • CCSS.ELA.W.9-10.5 — Develop and strengthen writing by planning, revising, editing, rewriting

📋 Materials

  • Famous constrained-creation examples (Twitter threads, haiku, 48-hour film festival)
  • Constraint cards
  • Project work in progress

🚀 Launch

SAY: “Twitter started with 140 characters. Haiku has 17 syllables. The Dogma film movement banned special effects and soundtracks. Every time a creator worked within a constraint, the result was more creative — not less.”

ASK: “Why would a limit make something more creative, not less?” (Take responses.)

📣 Teach It

TEACH 4 types of creative constraints:

1. Resource: Only 3 colors. Only recycled materials. Zero budget.

2. Time: Complete in 48 hours. Write in one sitting.

3. Format: No adjectives. Only images. Must rhyme.

4. Audience: Explain it to a 5-year-old. Write for someone who disagrees with you.

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Take a student's project idea and apply one constraint: “You can only use 10 words to describe the entire thing. Go.”

Show how the forced simplicity reveals the core of the idea.

SAY: “The constraint doesn’t ruin the idea. It clarifies it.”

📝 Practice

Students choose one constraint from the constraint card deck and apply it to one element of their current passion project. (e.g., 'Explain your project to an 8-year-old in 30 seconds' or 'Redesign your product with zero budget.') Sketch or write the constrained version. Reflect: what did the constraint reveal about your original plan?

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Choose constraint from a provided list of 5 (not open deck)

•  Apply constraint only to one small piece (not full project)

•  Partner thinks through the constraint together

Extensions

•  Apply 3 different constraints and compare results

•  Design a custom constraint for a classmate to apply to their project

•  Write a reflection on how professional creators in their field use constraints intentionally

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students ask AI: “Take my project idea [describe] and apply this constraint: [their chosen constraint]. What does the constrained version look like?”
  • Compare AI’s constrained version to theirs.
  • Discuss: “Does AI feel the difficulty of a constraint? Does that difference matter to the creative outcome?”

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students write: What did the constraint reveal about your project that you didn't know before?

Lesson 5: Creative Voice: Finding the Style That Is Unmistakably Yours

⏱  Duration: 25 min   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 11–12

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will analyze the elements of creative voice, identify their own stylistic tendencies, and intentionally apply their voice to a piece of capstone writing or design.

📚 Standards

  • CCSS.ELA.W.11-12.4 — Produce clear and coherent writing appropriate to task, purpose, audience

📋 Materials

  • 3 short excerpts from writers/designers with distinctive voice
  • Voice analysis framework
  • Students' own writing sample (paragraph from capstone)

🚀 Launch

SAY: “You can tell a Hemingway paragraph from a Toni Morrison paragraph before you even finish the first sentence. You can tell a Jony Ive product from a student project. Voice is the thing that makes it unmistakably YOURS.”

ASK: “Does your capstone sound like you? Or does it sound like 'a student report'?”

📣 Teach It

TEACH 5 elements of creative voice:

1. Sentence rhythm — short/punchy vs. long/flowing? Varied or consistent?

2. Word choice — plain and direct vs. precise and specific vs. metaphorical?

3. Point of view — how present is the creator in the work?

4. What you notice — the details you choose to include reveal your sensibility

5. What you risk — bold claims and unusual angles signal a distinctive voice

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Read two paragraphs on the same topic: one generic, one with distinctive voice.

Annotate: Where does the voice show up? (Circle a rhythm choice. Underline a word choice. Star a risk.)

SAY: “Generic writing sounds like anyone. Your writing should sound like no one but you.”

📝 Practice

Students analyze 2 of their own capstone paragraphs for voice using the 5-element framework. Identify: Where are you most present? Where do you disappear into generic academic language? Rewrite one paragraph to amplify voice while maintaining academic credibility.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Annotate teacher-provided excerpts before their own

•  Rewrite only 2–3 sentences, not a full paragraph

•  Identify one voice element to focus on

Extensions

•  Write a 'voice manifesto': 5 sentences describing their own creative aesthetic

•  Analyze the voice of their capstone product as a whole: does it sound like them?

•  Find a published piece with the kind of voice they aspire to and write a 1-page analysis

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students paste their paragraph into AI and ask: 'Describe the voice of this writing. Where is the author most present? Where do they disappear?'
  • Compare AI’s voice description to their own self-assessment.
  • Ask: 'Rewrite this paragraph to sound more distinctive without losing the argument.' Compare and evaluate: Did AI’s version sound more like them or less?
  • DISCUSS: “Can AI develop a creative voice? What does that mean for your work?”

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students highlight one sentence in their rewrite that they believe is the strongest example of their creative voice, and write one sentence explaining why.
