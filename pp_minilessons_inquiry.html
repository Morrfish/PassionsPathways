🔍  Inquiry & Questioning

Lesson 1: What is a Question?

⏱  Duration: 10 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: PreK–2

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will distinguish between questions and statements and practice asking their own 'I Wonder' questions.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.SL.K.1 — Participate in collaborative conversations
  • CCSS.ELA.SL.1.3 — Ask and answer questions about what a speaker says

📋 Materials Needed

  • Anchor chart paper
  • Markers
  • 'I Wonder' sentence strips for each student
  • Picture prompt cards (animals, space, weather, food)

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “Let me ask you something: Is a dog bigger than an ant?” Wait for responses.

SAY: “That thing I just said — that was a QUESTION. Questions are magic. They open doors to new things we don’t know yet.”

ASK: “Who can tell me one thing they wondered about when they woke up this morning?”

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH: Write on anchor chart: Questions start with WHO, WHAT, WHERE, WHEN, WHY, HOW.

SAY: “Statements tell us things. Questions ask us things. Let’s practice telling the difference.”

SHOW: Read pairs aloud: 'Dogs have four legs.' vs. 'How many legs does a dog have?' — students clap for the question.

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Hold up a picture card (e.g., a volcano).

SAY: “I wonder WHY a volcano shoots fire. I wonder HOW hot the lava is. I wonder IF I could ever get close to one safely.”

THINK ALOUD: “See how each of those started with a question word? That’s how wondering works!”

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Students choose a picture card. Each child says or dictates one 'I Wonder' question. Teacher or aide writes it on their sentence strip. Students decorate and post on the Wonder Wall.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Pre-printed sentence strips: 'I wonder ___'

•  Point to question-word chart while speaking

•  Partner shares first, then individual

Extensions

•  Write 3 different I Wonder questions for one picture

•  Sort questions by question word type

•  Interview a partner and record their question

🤖 AI Connection

  • Teacher models: “I’m going to ask AI 'What are 5 things a 5-year-old might wonder about the moon?'” Display results and discuss.
  • SAY: “AI can help us brainstorm questions too — but OUR questions about OUR lives are special!”

🚪 Exit Ticket

Each student points to their I Wonder sentence strip and says their question aloud.

Lesson 2: Noticing and Wondering: Looking Closely

⏱  Duration: 12 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: PreK–2

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will use observation skills to generate questions from careful looking at an object or image.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • NGSS K-2-ETS1-1 — Ask questions based on observations
  • CCSS.ELA.SL.K.4 — Describe familiar things with relevant details

📋 Materials Needed

  • 1 mystery object per table (sealed bag or box)
  • Magnifying glasses
  • Notice/Wonder T-chart on anchor chart
  • Paper for drawings

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “Scientists and artists and inventors all start the same way — they LOOK. Really, really carefully.”

SHOW a sealed bag with an object inside. SAY: “Don’t touch yet. Just look. What do you notice?”

Record 3–4 student observations on the chart.

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH: Draw T-chart: NOTICE (what I see) | WONDER (what I want to know).

SAY: “Noticing is what your eyes tell you. Wondering is what your brain asks next.”

PRACTICE round 1: Show an image (e.g., a deep-sea fish). Students say one NOTICE. Teacher records.

PRACTICE round 2: Students say one WONDER. Teacher records.

🎨 Model It

MODEL with a pinecone: “I notice it has brown scales. I notice it feels rough. I wonder if it always looked like this. I wonder what lives inside it.”

SAY: “Every notice can become a wonder. Watch: 'I notice it’s bumpy' becomes 'I wonder WHY it’s bumpy.'”

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Each table gets a mystery object. Students take turns: one notice, one wonder. Table recorder (teacher aide or older buddy) writes them down. Share out 1–2 per table.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Pre-labeled T-chart worksheet

•  Sentence stems: 'I notice ___ because my eyes see ___'

•  Objects chosen for strong tactile/visual features

Extensions

•  Write 5 notices AND 5 wonders

•  Sketch the object and label observations

•  Predict: what do you think the answer to your wonder is?

🤖 AI Connection

  • Teacher models: Type a notice into AI and ask 'What question could a curious 5-year-old ask about this?'
  • Discuss: “AI thought of ___ question. Did any of you think of the same one? A different one?”

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students point to the T-chart. Each says one thing they noticed AND one thing they wondered.

Lesson 3: My Wonder Wall: Building a Class Question Collection

⏱  Duration: 15 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: PreK–2

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will generate, organize, and display authentic curiosity questions on a shared classroom Wonder Wall.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.W.K.7 — Participate in shared research and writing
  • CCSS.ELA.SL.1.4 — Describe people, places, things, events with details

📋 Materials Needed

  • Large wall space or bulletin board
  • Sticky notes (large)
  • Markers
  • Category headers (Animals, Space, People, Food, How Things Work, My World)
  • 'I Wonder' stamps or stickers

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “We are going to build something amazing together. A wall of WONDER.”

SHOW: Point to blank wall/board. “Right now it’s empty. By the end of today, it will be FULL of your questions.”

ASK: “What’s one thing you’ve always wondered about? Take 10 seconds to think.”

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH: Show category headers. Explain each with an example question.

SAY: “When we organize questions, we can find them later. Scientists organize questions into categories too.”

SHOW: Model adding a question to the correct category: 'I wonder why fish can breathe underwater' → Animals section.

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Write 2 of your own wonder questions on sticky notes, thinking aloud about which category each belongs in.

SAY: “I wonder what’s at the bottom of the ocean. Let me think — that’s about animals AND water. I’ll put it under Animals.”

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Students write or dictate 1–2 wonder questions on sticky notes. They choose a category and post their question. Partners help each other with spelling or dictation. Teacher circulates and affirms questions.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Pre-written sentence starter sticky notes

•  Teacher scribes for non-writers

•  Can draw the question instead of writing

Extensions

•  Write 3 questions in different categories

•  Add a prediction below their wonder

•  Find a book or video that might answer one wonder

🤖 AI Connection

  • Teacher demonstration: “Watch me ask AI about one of our wonder questions.” Ask AI the question and display the response.
  • Discuss: “Is this a complete answer? What else would you still want to know?”
  • SAY: “AI can answer some wonders — but not all. Some questions only YOU can answer through experience.”

🚪 Exit Ticket

Each student reads or tells one wonder from the wall. Class gives a 'wonder hand' (wiggle fingers) for each one shared.

Lesson 4: Big Questions vs. Small Questions

⏱  Duration: 10 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: PreK–2

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will distinguish between narrow one-fact questions and open questions that lead to exploration and deeper learning.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.RI.2.1 — Ask and answer such questions as who, what, where, when, why and how

📋 Materials Needed

  • Two-column chart: SMALL vs. BIG questions
  • Question example cards
  • Thumbs up/thumbs down cards

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “Some questions are like a tiny box — you open it, find one thing, and you’re done.”

SAY: “Some questions are like a treasure map — following them takes you on an adventure!”

ASK: “Which kind of question do you think we want for learning?”

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH: Show two-column chart. Explain: Small questions = one word or number answer. Big questions = take you on a journey.

SHOW pairs:

   Small: 'What color is a flamingo?' → Pink. Done.

   Big: 'Why are flamingos pink and what would happen if they ate different food?'

Small: 'How many planets are there?' → 8. Done.

Big: 'Could humans ever live on another planet and what would that be like?'

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Take a topic (e.g., dogs) and show making it small vs. big.

SAY: “Small: How many legs does a dog have? — now I know. Big: How do dogs learn to understand human words? — NOW I want to find out more!”

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Give each pair a question card. They sort it: Big or Small? Show thumbs up for Big, thumbs down for Small. Discuss: 'How could you make this small question bigger?'

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Only sort — don't create new questions

•  Pre-paired with a partner for discussion

•  Question cards with simple illustrations

Extensions

•  Take a small question and rewrite it as a big question

•  Create your own small AND big question pair

•  Predict: what might you find out if you explored the big question?

🤖 AI Connection

  • Teacher asks AI a small question, then a big question on the same topic.
  • Compare the responses: 'Which answer was longer? More interesting? More useful for a project?'

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students give thumbs up (big) or thumbs down (small) as teacher reads 3 new questions aloud.

Lesson 5: From Curiosity to Research Question

⏱  Duration: 15 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 3–5

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will transform a vague curiosity into a focused, researchable driving question using a 3-step refinement process.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.W.4.7 — Conduct short research projects
  • CCSS.ELA.W.5.7 — Recall and gather information from multiple sources

📋 Materials Needed

  • Curiosity → Question funnel graphic organizer
  • Sample curiosity statements
  • Anchor chart: Too broad / Too narrow / Just right

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “You know what’s interesting about curiosity? It’s kind of messy. 'I love space' isn’t something you can research. But 'I wonder if there’s water on Mars and whether humans could survive there' — NOW we’re talking.”

ASK: “What’s something you’re curious about right now? Just blurt it out — even if it’s vague.” Take 3–4 responses.

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH: Show funnel graphic. Curiosity goes in at the top, focused question comes out at the bottom.

Step 1 — Start with a broad interest: 'I like dogs'

Step 2 — Add a specific angle: 'I’m curious about how dogs communicate'

Step 3 — Turn into a driving question: 'How do dogs use body language to tell humans what they need?'

SHOW anchor chart: Too broad (How do animals work?) / Too narrow (What color is a golden retriever?) / Just right.

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Start with 'I like video games.'

Step 1: 'I like video games' → Step 2: 'I’m curious about how games are made' → Step 3: 'What skills do game designers need, and how do they learn them?'

SAY: “Notice it’s specific enough to research, but big enough that there’s a LOT I could find out.”

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Students start with their own blurted curiosity. Using the funnel graphic, they write Curiosity → Specific Angle → Driving Question. Partners swap and give feedback: Is it too broad, too narrow, or just right?

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Pre-filled Step 1 and 2, student only writes Step 3

•  Provide 5 sample curiosities to choose from

•  Sentence frames: 'What would happen if...' / 'How does... affect...'

Extensions

•  Generate 3 different driving questions from one curiosity

•  Evaluate which question would lead to the most interesting project

•  Write a paragraph explaining why their driving question matters to them

🤖 AI Connection

  • Student use: “Type your interest into AI and ask it to suggest 5 possible driving questions.”
  • Then: “Choose the best one, explain why, and check: is it too broad, too narrow, or just right?”
  • DISCUSS: “Did AI’s suggestions surprise you? Did you like yours or AI’s better? Why?”

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students share their final driving question. Partners rate: thumbs up (just right), hand flat (broad), pinched fingers (too narrow).

Lesson 6: Asking Sub-Questions: Mapping What You Need to Know

⏱  Duration: 15 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 3–5

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will break a driving question into 4–6 sub-questions that will guide their research and ensure comprehensive coverage of the topic.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.RI.4.1 — Refer to details and examples in a text when explaining what the text says
  • CCSS.ELA.W.5.7 — Conduct short research projects using several sources

📋 Materials Needed

  • Sub-question web graphic organizer
  • Sample driving question on board
  • Anchor chart: Types of sub-questions (background, cause, effect, comparison, future)

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “Imagine you’re about to take a road trip. You know where you’re going. But you still need to know: Which route? Where do we stop? What do we pack? Your driving question is the destination. Sub-questions are your map.”

SHOW the driving question on the board: 'How do sharks hunt their prey?'

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH 5 types of sub-questions:

Background: 'What do sharks eat and where do they live?' (What do I need to know first?)

Cause: 'What triggers a shark to hunt?' (Why does this happen?)

Effect: 'How does shark hunting affect ocean ecosystems?' (What happens as a result?)

Comparison: 'How do great whites hunt differently from hammerheads?' (How is this different?)

Future: 'Will climate change affect how sharks hunt?' (What might happen?)

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Place driving question in the center of the web. Generate one sub-question of each type aloud, writing on the web.

SAY: “Each sub-question is like a chapter in my research. Together they help me cover the whole topic, not just one part.”

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Students take their own driving question and fill in a sub-question web: one of each type (background, cause, effect, comparison, future). Share with a partner. Partner adds one question they think is missing.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Pre-filled web with sub-question type labels

•  Provide the driving question (use class topic)

•  Only require 3 sub-questions instead of 5

Extensions

•  Rank sub-questions by research priority

•  Predict which will be hardest to find information about

•  Turn sub-questions into a table of contents for the project

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students prompt AI: “Ask AI: 'Generate 6 sub-questions for my driving question: [their question]'”
  • Compare AI-generated list to their own. Add any types they missed. Remove any that are too narrow.
  • Discuss: “Did AI think of a type of question you hadn’t considered?”

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students share one sub-question and identify its type. Class confirms type using anchor chart.

Lesson 7: Evaluating Questions: What Makes a Question Worth Researching?

⏱  Duration: 15 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 3–5

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will apply a 4-criterion rubric to evaluate whether a research question is worthy of a full passion project.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.W.4.7 — Recall and gather information from experiences or print and digital sources
  • CCSS.ELA.SL.4.1 — Engage effectively in collaborative discussions

📋 Materials Needed

  • Question evaluation rubric (printed)
  • Sample questions on question cards (12–15)
  • Whiteboard and markers

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “Not every question is worth a 4-week project. Some are too easy. Some are unanswerable. Great researchers test their questions before they commit to them.”

ASK: “Have you ever started to research something and then run out of things to find? What happened?”

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH 4 criteria for a great research question:

1. FINDABLE — Can I actually find information about this? (Not 'What is my dog thinking?')

2. FOCUSED — Is it specific enough to research fully in the time I have?

3. INTERESTING — Does it genuinely excite me enough to stay curious for weeks?

4. IMPACTFUL — Would knowing the answer matter to someone besides just me?

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Rate a sample question using the 4-criterion rubric (score 1–3 on each criterion).

Question: 'What are all the animals in the world?' — Findable (3), Focused (1), Interesting (2), Impactful (1) — Total 7/12 — needs revision.

Improved: 'How do endangered rainforest animals adapt when their habitat shrinks?' — rate again.

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Small groups each get 3 question cards. They rate each using the rubric. Then rank their 3 questions: best to least research-worthy. Share rankings with class and defend choices.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Rate only 1–2 criteria instead of all 4

•  Work in pairs instead of small group

•  Pre-rated examples to compare against

Extensions

•  Take a low-scoring question and rewrite it to score 10+/12

•  Create their own ‘bad’ question and challenge classmates to improve it

•  Write a defense paragraph for why their chosen question deserves a project

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students ask AI to rate one of their question cards using the 4 criteria.
  • Compare AI’s rating to their own. Where did they agree? Disagree?
  • Discuss: “Should AI decide if your question is worth researching? Why or why not?”

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students write their driving question and give it a self-score on all 4 criteria. Identify which criterion needs the most work.

Lesson 8: The Wonder Web: Connecting Questions Across Topics

⏱  Duration: 15 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 3–5

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will discover how a single passion connects to multiple subject areas by mapping a 'Wonder Web' of cross-curricular questions.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.RI.3.3 — Describe the relationship between a series of historical events or concepts
  • CCSS.ELA.W.4.7 — Conduct short research projects

📋 Materials Needed

  • Wonder Web blank template (central topic + 6 radiating subject areas)
  • Color markers
  • Subject area icons (science, math, history, art, PE, technology)

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “Here’s a secret most people don’t know: every passion connects to EVERY subject. Let me prove it.”

Write SOCCER in the center of a web. SAY: “Watch.”

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH: Connect SOCCER to each subject:

Science: How does the curved kick work? (physics of spin)

Math: What statistics matter most in soccer? (angles, probability)

History: How did soccer spread around the world? (colonialism, culture)

Art: How do stadiums get designed? (architecture, design)

Health/PE: What training prevents injuries? (biomechanics)

Technology: How do analytics change strategy? (data science)

SAY: “Your passion isn’t just ONE thing. It’s a doorway to everything.”

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Start a new Wonder Web with COOKING in the center. Fill in 3 subject connections aloud.

SAY: “Now you’ll try with YOUR passion. See how many subjects you can connect.”

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Students put their passion in the center. Fill in subject connections with one question per subject. Try to connect to at least 4 different subjects. Share the most surprising connection with a partner.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Provide subject-area question starters for each category

•  Use a class topic (e.g., weather) for whole-group version

•  Allow 3 connections instead of 4–6

Extensions

•  Create a Wonder Web with 8+ subject connections

•  Choose the most surprising connection and develop it into a driving question

•  Explain how two different subject connections might interact

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students prompt AI: “How does [passion] connect to math? Science? History?”
  • Add any new connections AI suggests to their web.
  • Discuss: “Did AI make a connection you wouldn’t have thought of? Was it accurate?”

Lesson 9: The Art of the Driving Question

⏱  Duration: 20 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 6–8

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will analyze the characteristics of powerful driving questions and construct an original driving question that meets all four quality criteria for a multi-week passion project.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.W.6.7 — Conduct short research projects to answer a question
  • CCSS.ELA.W.7.7 — Conduct short research projects, drawing on several sources
  • CCSS.ELA.W.8.7 — Conduct short research projects to answer a question, drawing on several sources and generating additional related, focused questions

📋 Materials Needed

  • Driving question analysis rubric
  • 15 sample driving questions at varying quality levels
  • Whiteboard or projector

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “The best research projects in history — the ones that changed how we understand the world — started with a question so good that the researcher couldn’t sleep until they answered it. We’re going to learn how to write one of those.”

SHOW: Two questions side by side. 'What is climate change?' vs. 'Who bears the most responsibility for climate change — individuals, corporations, or governments — and does that responsibility shift when we look at historical vs. current emissions?'

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH 4 hallmarks of a powerful driving question:

1. ARGUABLE — Reasonable people could disagree about the answer

2. RESEARCHABLE — Evidence actually exists to help answer it

3. FOCUSED — Specific enough to answer in the project time frame

4. MEANINGFUL — The answer matters to someone beyond just the researcher

ANALYZE 3 sample questions using the 4 hallmarks together:

Weak: 'What are sharks?' — Not arguable, barely researchable

Okay: 'Why are shark populations declining?' — Researchable and focused, somewhat meaningful

Strong: 'To what extent are current ocean conservation policies effective at reversing shark population decline, and who bears the political cost of stronger protections?' — All 4 hallmarks

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Start with topic 'music.' Build question in stages: music → music and mental health → Can music therapy reduce anxiety as effectively as medication for teenagers, and why is it underutilized in schools?

SAY: “Notice how each step adds specificity, an arguable angle, and makes the stakes real.”

Students take their passion topic through the same staged refinement process. Write 3 progressive versions of a driving question (increasingly stronger). Partner evaluates each version against the 4 hallmarks. Choose the strongest for their project.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Topic starter list with 20 passion areas

•  Sentence frames: 'To what extent does... affect...?' / 'Who bears the greatest responsibility for...?'

•  Analyze 2 hallmarks only instead of 4

Extensions

•  Write the counterargument to their driving question

•  Identify what type of evidence would most powerfully answer it

•  Find an existing published study that asks a similar question

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students prompt AI: “Take my driving question [paste it] and score it 1–3 on each hallmark: arguable, researchable, focused, meaningful. Explain your reasoning.”
  • Compare AI’s score to your own. Then ask AI: 'How could I make this question stronger?'
  • Evaluate AI’s suggestions: Use what improves your question. Reject what doesn’t fit your passion.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.SL.6.4 — Present claims and findings using appropriate eye contact, adequate volume, and clear pronunciation

TEACH 3 question types:

Open: Invites story, explanation, or reflection. 'Can you describe a time when...' 'What has surprised you most about...'

Scaffolds

•  Pre-written question starters by type

•  Interview 3 questions instead of 6

•  Partner plays a provided character with known answers

•  Conduct an actual interview outside of class; transcribe and annotate it

  • Add any strong AI-generated questions to their list, marked as AI-assisted in their log.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.RI.6.8 — Trace and evaluate the argument and specific claims in a text

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH lateral reading steps:

Step 1: Don’t start reading the article yet.

Step 2: Open a new tab. Search the SOURCE (not the topic): 'Who is [author/organization]?'

Step 4: Now decide: is this source credible enough to use?

Step 5: Check: What is their funding, bias, or agenda?

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Show a convincing-looking website on a research topic.

SAY: 'My first instinct is to read it. But watch — I’m going to search the organization’s name instead.'

Open a new tab, search the organization, find what others say.

SAY: 'Look — this source is funded by an industry group with a clear financial interest in this topic. Would I use it?'

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Students receive 3 sources on the same topic. Apply lateral reading to each: 5 minutes per source. Fill in T-chart. Decide: trustworthy, use with caution, or avoid. Compare decisions with a partner and discuss any disagreements.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Pre-identified the one credible source — students only evaluate the other two

•  Provide search terms to use when looking up each source

•  Fill in T-chart together as a class for first source

Extensions

•  Find an example of the same claim made by 3 sources of varying credibility

•  Write a paragraph explaining how the credibility difference changed the information

•  Create a 'Source Evaluation Guide' for a younger class

  • Students ask AI: “Is [source name] generally considered credible? What is their bias or funding?”
  • CRITICAL DISCUSSION: Can AI itself be evaluated for credibility? Who funds AI? What are its biases?
  • SAY: 'You should lateral read AI the same way you lateral read any source — by checking its claims against other sources.'

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students rate all 3 sources (trustworthy/caution/avoid) and write one sentence explaining their most important lateral reading finding.

Lesson 13: Constructing a Thesis: From Question to Arguable Claim

⏱  Duration: 25 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 9–10

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will differentiate between a research topic, a research question, and a thesis claim, then construct a thesis that is specific, arguable, and evidence-supported.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.W.9-10.1 — Write arguments to support claims
  • CCSS.ELA.W.9-10.7 — Conduct short as well as more sustained research projects

📋 Materials Needed

  • Thesis quality spectrum chart
  • Sample thesis statements (10 examples at varying quality)
  • Color-coded highlighters (green/yellow/red)

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “A thesis is not a fact. It is not a question. It is not a summary. It is a claim that someone reasonable could disagree with — and that you can prove with evidence.”

ASK: “Is this a thesis: 'Climate change is a problem'?” Discuss. “Is this: 'Federal climate policy consistently favors corporate economic interests over rural community climate resilience'?” Why?

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH 4 thesis failure modes:

1. TOO OBVIOUS: 'War is bad.' No one disagrees.

2. TOO VAGUE: 'Social media affects teenagers.' How? In what way? So what?

3. JUST A FACT: 'The 1964 Civil Rights Act prohibited discrimination.' Not arguable.

4. TOO BROAD: 'The economy has many problems.' Covers everything, argues nothing.

TEACH the thesis upgrade formula:

Start with: Topic [What are you writing about?]

Add: Claim [What do you argue is true about it?]

Add: Significance [Why does this matter / what does it mean?]

Optional: Stakes [What happens if we don’t acknowledge this?]

🎨 Model It

MODEL: 'Social media' → 'Social media platforms deliberately exploit adolescent psychological vulnerabilities to maximize engagement' → 'which means the industry, not individuals, bears primary responsibility for teen mental health outcomes.'

SAY: “Notice: arguable (industry vs. individual debate is real), specific (adolescent psychology, engagement design), and meaningful (policy implications).”

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Students write 3 versions of a thesis for their project topic: weak, moderate, and strong. Highlight each using the quality spectrum criteria. Partner workshop: trade papers, use color coding to rate each other's strong thesis, suggest one improvement.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Thesis sentence frame: 'Although many believe ___, [topic] actually ___ because ___, which matters because ___.'

•  Rate only weak vs. strong (2 versions instead of 3)

•  Use a class-shared topic for practice

Extensions

•  Write a counterargument paragraph responding to their strong thesis

•  Find 3 published scholarly articles whose thesis matches theirs in complexity

•  Identify how the thesis would change if one piece of evidence were removed

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students paste their thesis into AI and ask: 'Score this thesis 1–3 on: arguability, specificity, and significance. Explain your score and suggest one improvement.'
  • Critically evaluate AI’s suggestion: Does it make the thesis stronger or more generic?
  • DISCUSSION: 'Could AI write a strong thesis for you? And if so, would it be YOUR thesis?'

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students submit one thesis statement and a 2-sentence rationale: what makes it arguable, and what evidence will support it.

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will identify six forms of bias in research sources and evaluate how bias affects the reliability and utility of evidence for their projects.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.RI.9-10.6 — Determine an author’s point of view or purpose and analyze how the author uses rhetoric

📋 Materials Needed

  • Six-bias reference chart
  • 2 articles on the same topic from opposite ends of the bias spectrum
  • Bias detection annotation protocol

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “Every source has a perspective. That doesn’t automatically make it useless — but it means you need to know what lens you’re reading through before you trust what you’re seeing.”

ASK: “If a tobacco company funds a study that says cigarettes are safe, does the study become wrong? Or just less trustworthy? What’s the difference?”

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH 6 forms of bias:

1. Funding bias — Who paid for this? (Industry-funded studies favor industry outcomes)

2. Selection bias — Who was included/excluded from the sample?

3. Confirmation bias — Did the researcher only look for evidence that confirms their existing view?

4. Publication bias — Only 'positive' study results get published; negatives disappear

5. Framing bias — Same facts, very different headlines/emphasis

6. Omission bias — What is NOT mentioned that should be?

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Annotate one article using the bias detection protocol: underline loaded language, box uncertain claims, note funding source in margin.

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Students receive 2 articles on the same topic. Annotate each for bias using the protocol. Build a comparison chart: What does each article emphasize? Omit? Who wrote it? Who benefits from its conclusions? Write a 1-paragraph synthesis: What does a researcher who has read BOTH know that a researcher who read only one doesn't?

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Annotate for 3 bias types instead of 6

•  Pre-highlighted examples in each article

•  Fill in a structured chart with sentence frames

Extensions

•  Research who funded each article and write a source credibility brief

•  Find a third source that sits between the two and analyze what it omits from each

•  Explain how bias in sources requires adjusting the language of your own claims

  • Students ask AI: 'Describe the typical bias pattern of [source type: news outlet, government report, industry study, advocacy group].'

  • Discuss: What is AI’s own bias? What perspectives is it more likely to represent?

Lesson 15: Primary Research Design: Surveys, Interviews, and Observations

⏱  Duration: 25 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 9–10

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will design a primary research instrument (survey, interview, or observation protocol) appropriate to their project's research question.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.W.9-10.7 — Conduct short as well as more sustained research projects to answer a question

📋 Materials Needed

  • Research design comparison chart (survey vs. interview vs. observation)
  • Survey design checklist
  • Sample research instruments for analysis

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “Secondary research tells you what others have found. Primary research lets you find something NEW — something that exists nowhere in the world until you go get it.”

ASK: “If no one has studied your exact question, what would you actually DO to find the answer?” Take 2–3 responses.

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH 3 primary research methods:

Survey: Best for gathering data from many people quickly. Risk: leading questions, low response rates, shallow data.

Interview: Best for depth, nuance, and unexpected insights. Risk: time-intensive, small sample, interviewer bias.

Observation: Best for behavior, environment, or process. Risk: observer effect, time, interpretation bias.

RULE: The research question should determine the method, not convenience.

TEACH survey design principles:

1. Neutral language — Avoid loading the question toward an answer

2. One idea per question — Never double-barrel ('Did you feel safe and comfortable?')

3. Include 'N/A' or 'Prefer not to answer' where relevant

4. Test the survey yourself before sending

5. Sample size matters — small samples need qualitative follow-up

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Show a poorly designed survey. Identify 3 problems using the checklist.

Rewrite 2 of the problem questions live, thinking aloud about the improvements.

SAY: “Every question should be answerable by your target respondent in under 30 seconds.”

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Students choose a primary research method for their project. Design 5–6 survey questions OR 4–5 interview questions OR an observation protocol. Apply the design checklist. Pilot with one partner as respondent. Identify 2 revisions needed.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Use the survey method (most structured)

•  Question stems provided for each question type

•  Design 3 questions instead of 5–6

Extensions

•  Design all 3 instrument types for the same research question; compare what each would reveal

•  Write an IRB-style brief explaining ethical considerations of your research design

•  Calculate the minimum sample size for statistical significance using an online calculator

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students ask AI: 'Review my survey questions [paste them]. Identify any leading, double-barreled, or ambiguous questions and suggest improvements.'
  • Evaluate AI’s suggestions carefully: Does the revised version actually change the meaning?
  • Discuss: 'If AI can design a survey, what value does human judgment add to research design?'

Lesson 16: Synthesis Thinking: Connecting Evidence Across Sources

⏱  Duration: 25 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 9–10

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will practice academic synthesis by identifying patterns, contradictions, and gaps across multiple sources and constructing an original analytical insight.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.W.9-10.8 — Gather relevant information from multiple authoritative print and digital sources

📋 Materials Needed

  • 4 short excerpts from different sources on a shared topic
  • Synthesis matrix graphic organizer
  • Color highlighters (one per source)

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “Summary says what each source argues. Synthesis asks: what do ALL these sources, taken together, reveal that none of them says alone?”

SAY: “It’s the difference between introducing guests at a party and facilitating an actual conversation between them.”

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH 4 synthesis moves:

1. Convergence: 'Sources A, B, and C all agree that..., which strongly suggests...'

2. Divergence: 'While Source A argues..., Source B contends..., revealing a fundamental disagreement about...'

3. Gap: 'None of the sources address..., which is significant because...'

4. Extension: 'Taking Sources A and B together, we can infer something neither explicitly states: ...'

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Show 2 excerpts on the same topic. Use the matrix to map what each says, then write a convergence sentence and a divergence sentence.

SAY: “The synthesis isn’t in the sources. It’s in YOUR thinking.”

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Students receive 4 excerpts. Fill in the synthesis matrix for each source. Identify: 1 convergence, 1 divergence, 1 gap, 1 extension. Write a 150-word synthesis paragraph using at least 3 of the 4 moves.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Use 2 sources instead of 4

•  Pre-fill the matrix for 2 of the sources

•  Provide synthesis sentence starters for each move type

Extensions

•  Add a 5th source that changes the synthesis

•  Write a second synthesis paragraph using only the sources that contradict each other

•  Identify what type of additional evidence would resolve the divergence

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students paste all 4 source excerpts into AI and ask: 'What patterns do you notice across these sources? What do they agree on? Disagree on? What is missing?'
  • Compare AI’s synthesis to theirs: Does AI notice anything they missed? Did AI miss anything they caught?
  • Discuss: 'If AI can synthesize sources, what uniquely human insight does your synthesis add?'

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students write one original synthesis sentence using the 'extension' move: what can be inferred that no single source explicitly states?

Lesson 17: Literature Review: Mapping the Conversation

⏱  Duration: 30 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 11–12

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will construct a structured literature review that positions their capstone research within the existing scholarly conversation on their topic.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.W.11-12.7 — Conduct sustained research projects
  • CCSS.ELA.W.11-12.8 — Gather relevant information from multiple authoritative sources

📋 Materials Needed

  • Annotated bibliography (minimum 5 sources per student, pre-assigned)
  • Literature review structure template
  • Synthesis matrix

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “A literature review doesn’t summarize sources one by one. It maps the CONVERSATION. It shows: here’s what scholars agree on, here’s where they disagree, and here’s the gap my work will fill.”

SAY: “Your capstone enters a room where a conversation has been going on. A literature review shows you know what’s been said — and earns you the right to add your voice.”

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH 4-part literature review structure:

Part 1: Overview — What is the landscape of research on this topic? (2–3 sentences)

Part 2: Key Themes — What are the 2–3 major findings or arguments scholars have made? (1 paragraph per theme)

Part 3: Debates — Where do scholars disagree? Why? What evidence drives each side? (1 paragraph)

Part 4: Gap — What has not been studied? What does your capstone address that existing work doesn’t? (1 paragraph)

TEACH literature review writing conventions:

Present tense for ongoing consensus: 'Researchers argue that...'

Past tense for specific historical findings: 'Smith (2018) found that...'

Group sources thematically, not one by one

Never start a paragraph with a citation — start with the IDEA, then support with sources

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Write the first 2 sentences of a literature review on a class-familiar topic (e.g., project-based learning).

Show: how to group 3 sources around one theme rather than summarizing each separately.

SAY: “The reader shouldn’t be able to tell where one source ends and another begins. The theme is primary; the sources are evidence.”

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Students draft Part 1 (overview) and Part 4 (gap) of their literature review using their annotated bibliography. Partner review: Does the gap clearly explain what the capstone adds to the existing conversation? Revise based on feedback.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Template with sentence frames for each part

•  Use 3 sources instead of 5–6

•  Teacher pre-identifies the major theme groupings from their bibliography

Extensions

•  Draft all 4 parts

•  Add a section on methodological differences across studies

•  Situate the capstone within a specific school of thought or theoretical framework

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students paste their annotated bibliography into AI and ask: 'Identify 3 thematic patterns across these sources. What do they collectively argue? What question do they leave unanswered?'
  • Compare AI’s themes to their own groupings. Adjust if AI identified something more precise.
  • Discuss: 'Can AI write a literature review for you? What would it miss about your own capstone’s specific contribution?'

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students write the gap statement for their literature review: what their capstone adds to the existing scholarship, in 3–4 sentences.

Lesson 18: Research Ethics: Responsibility, Consent, and Integrity

⏱  Duration: 25 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 11–12

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will identify ethical obligations in research involving human subjects and AI, and apply ethical decision-making to their capstone research design.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.W.11-12.7 — Conduct sustained research projects
  • APA Ethics Code / IRB principles (simplified)

📋 Materials Needed

  • Research ethics case studies (4 scenarios)
  • Ethics decision matrix
  • Capstone research design document

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “Research has done remarkable good and terrible harm. The Tuskegee experiment. The Nazi medical trials. The Facebook emotion study. All were conducted by researchers who told themselves they had good reasons.”

ASK: “What makes research ethical? Is it the intention? The outcome? The process? Or all three?”

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH 5 core research ethics principles:

1. Informed consent — Participants must understand what they’re participating in

2. Confidentiality — Personal data must be protected

3. Do no harm — Physical, psychological, social, or reputational harm

4. Honesty — Report findings accurately, even if they contradict your hypothesis

5. Credit — Acknowledge all sources, collaborators, and tools used (including AI)

TEACH AI ethics in research:

Using AI to generate data is fabrication

Using AI to summarize sources requires verification

AI outputs must be credited in the AI Use Statement

AI bias affects the validity of AI-assisted analysis

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Apply ethics principles to a capstone scenario: 'A student interviews community members about neighborhood crime. What ethical obligations arise?'

Work through consent, confidentiality, potential harm, and reporting obligations aloud.

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Groups receive ethics case studies (4 scenarios: survey, interview, experiment, AI-assisted). Apply the 5-principle matrix to each. Identify: Is this ethical as described? What would need to change? Present findings to class.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Focus on 3 principles instead of 5

•  Scenario cards with pre-identified ethical issues; students name the principle violated

•  Use only their own capstone scenario

Extensions

•  Write a formal IRB-style research ethics brief for their capstone

•  Research a famous case of research misconduct and analyze which principles were violated

•  Design an informed consent form for their primary research

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students ask AI: 'What are the ethical considerations I should be aware of when conducting [their specific research method] on [their specific topic]?'
  • CRITICAL DISCUSSION: Does AI itself operate ethically? Who consented to the data used to train it? Who benefits from its outputs?
  • Students write their AI Ethics Reflection: 'How will I use AI responsibly in my capstone?'

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students identify one ethical obligation specific to their capstone research and write one concrete action they will take to honor it.

Lesson 19: Advanced Synthesis: Building an Original Argument from Evidence

⏱  Duration: 30 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 11–12

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will construct a multi-source analytical argument that synthesizes evidence into an original scholarly claim supported by their capstone research.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.W.11-12.1 — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis
  • CCSS.ELA.W.11-12.8 — Gather relevant information from multiple authoritative sources

📋 Materials Needed

  • Students' own annotated bibliographies
  • Argument construction scaffold
  • Peer review protocol

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “The most powerful thing a researcher can do is take evidence no one has combined in quite this way before and argue something that follows from it. That’s what distinguishes scholarship from reporting.”

SAY: “Today you’re going to build the argument that will sit at the center of your capstone.”

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH the 5-layer argument structure:

Layer 1: CLAIM — The arguable assertion you are making

Layer 2: EVIDENCE — What specific sources support it? (cite with page/section)

Layer 3: REASONING — Why does this evidence support this claim? (the logic bridge)

Layer 4: COUNTERARGUMENT — What would someone who disagrees say?

Layer 5: REBUTTAL — Why does your evidence still hold despite that counterargument?

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Build a 5-layer argument using 2 sources on a class-familiar topic.

Show how the reasoning layer is often skipped — and why that makes arguments weak.

SAY: “Evidence doesn’t argue. You argue. The evidence is your proof. But you are the one making the case.”

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Students build a 5-layer argument for the central claim of their capstone using 3–4 sources from their bibliography. Peer workshop: partner identifies the weakest layer and explains why. Revise that layer before submitting.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Sentence frames for each layer

•  Use 2 sources instead of 3–4

•  Pre-written evidence selections; student adds reasoning and rebuttal only

Extensions

•  Build a second 5-layer argument that supports a different aspect of the thesis

•  Identify what new evidence would strengthen the weakest layer

•  Write a full 2-page argument section using this structure

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students paste their 5-layer argument into AI and ask: 'Score each layer 1–3 on strength. Identify the weakest layer and explain why.'
  • Compare AI’s assessment to the partner’s feedback.
  • Ask: 'What is the strongest counterargument to my claim? How would I rebut it?'
  • Students revise their rebuttal layer based on the strongest counterargument AI identified.

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students submit their 5-layer argument with the weakest layer highlighted and a plan for strengthening it.

Lesson 20: Defending Your Thinking: Preparing for the Capstone Panel Q&A

⏱  Duration: 30 minutes   🎓  Grade Band: Grades 11–12

🎯 Lesson Objective

Students will anticipate challenging panel questions about their capstone and develop confident, evidence-based responses using an academic defense framework.

📚 Standards Alignment

  • CCSS.ELA.SL.11-12.4 — Present information clearly, concisely, and logically
  • CCSS.ELA.SL.11-12.6 — Adapt speech to a variety of contexts and tasks

📋 Materials Needed

  • Q&A preparation framework
  • Panel question bank (30 challenging questions by category)
  • Partner practice protocol

🚀 Lesson Launch (2-3 min)

SAY: “The Q&A is not an attack. It is an invitation to think out loud in front of experts. The students who do best are not the ones with all the answers — they’re the ones who know how to think through a hard question gracefully.”

SAY: “Today we practice exactly that.”

📣 Teach It (Direct Instruction)

TEACH 5 categories of panel questions:

1. Clarification: 'Can you explain what you mean by...?'

2. Evidence challenge: 'What evidence supports that specific claim?'

3. Alternative: 'Have you considered that the opposite might be true?'

4. Implication: 'If your findings are correct, what would that mean for...?'

5. Personal: 'What did you personally learn from this that surprised you?'

TEACH the EPIC response framework:

E — Engage: Acknowledge the question: 'That’s an important challenge...'

P — Position: State your position clearly: 'My project argues that...'

I — Illustrate: Cite one specific piece of evidence: 'In my research, I found...'

C — Connect: Tie it back to your thesis: '...which supports my central claim that...'

🎨 Model It

MODEL: Take a challenging question from the bank. Answer it poorly (flustered, vague). Then answer using EPIC.

SAY: “Notice: the content was the same. The EPIC structure made it sound like scholarship instead of guesswork.”

📝 Practice (Student Work Time)

Students select 5 questions from the panel question bank that they most dread. Write EPIC responses for each. Practice with a partner acting as a tough panelist. Partner provides 1 strength and 1 improvement per response.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Select 3 questions instead of 5

•  Use EPIC sentence starters for each section

•  Record responses and listen back for confidence and clarity

Extensions

•  Write responses to all 30 questions in the bank

•  Create 5 questions you HOPE the panel asks and 5 you dread most; compare them

•  Design your own Q&A question bank for a younger class's capstone

🤖 AI Connection

  • Students ask AI: 'I’m defending a capstone about [topic]. What are the 5 most challenging questions an expert panel might ask?'
  • Add any strong AI-generated questions to their practice list.
  • Ask AI: 'Play the role of a skeptical panelist and ask me your hardest question about [thesis].' Practice responding live.
  • Reflect: 'Were AI’s questions harder or easier than the ones I feared most?'

🚪 Exit Ticket

Students identify their most difficult anticipated question and write a full EPIC response that they feel confident delivering.
