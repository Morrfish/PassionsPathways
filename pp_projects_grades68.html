Grades 6–8

DESIGN Stage

Planning, researching, building, and revising multi-week passion projects

Structure Note: Grades 6–8 projects use formal proposal systems, multi-source research, iterative revision cycles, and structured AI logs. Reflection is written in paragraph form. Products are polished and presented to authentic audiences. Design thinking vocabulary is introduced explicitly.

Project 1: Solve a Real Problem in Our School

⏱  Time Frame: 4–5 weeks

❓  Driving Question: How can we identify a genuine problem in our school and design an evidence-based solution that actually helps people?

🎯 Learning Goals

  • Use design thinking to identify, research, and address a real problem
  • Collect and analyze primary data (surveys, interviews, observations)
  • Prototype a solution and test it against criteria
  • Present findings and recommendations to a real decision-maker (principal, staff)

📅 Daily Breakdown

Day(s)

Focus

Activities

Days 1–2

Empathize & Define

• Problem walk: observe school for 20 minutes and document issues

• Empathy interview: talk to 2 different people affected by a problem

• Define: Write a clear problem statement using HMW (“How Might We...”) framing

Days 3–5

Research Phase

• Research existing solutions (nationally, globally) for similar problems

• Survey at least 15 students or staff about the problem

• Analyze survey data and identify top findings

Days 6–8

Ideate & Plan

• Brainstorm 10+ solutions (no judgment)

• Filter using criteria: impact, feasibility, cost, time

• Select best solution and write a formal proposal

Days 9–12

Build Prototype

• Build physical or digital prototype

• Test with 3–5 real users; collect structured feedback

• Revise prototype based on testing data

Days 13–15

Pitch & Reflect

• Present to principal, staff member, or school board representative

• Respond to real questions and feedback

• Reflection essay and portfolio capture

🏫 Scripted Mini-Lesson: How Might We (HMW) Problem Framing

Day 2 — 20 minutes

•  SAY: “Designers don’t just say ‘that’s a problem.’ They turn problems into opportunities using a special sentence: How Might We...”

•  EXAMPLE: Problem: “The cafeteria is too loud.”

•     Too narrow HMW: “How might we make the cafeteria quieter?” (limits solutions)

•     Too broad HMW: “How might we fix all communication at school?” (too vague)

•     Just right: “How might we create a calmer lunchtime experience for all students?”

•  PRACTICE: Students take their identified problem and write 3 different HMW versions.

•  SHARE & RANK: Small groups vote on which HMW is best and most actionable.

•  DEBRIEF: The HMW statement becomes the core of their project proposal.

🤖 AI Integration

Approved AI Uses (Student-Facing with Logged Use)

•  Brainstorming: “Generate 15 possible solutions for [HMW statement].” Evaluate and select best 3.

•  Survey design: “Help me write 6 clear survey questions about [problem]. Check for leading language.” Review and revise.

•  Data analysis: “Here is my survey data [paste results]. What patterns do you notice?” Students verify patterns independently.

•  Pitch practice: “Play the role of a skeptical principal. Ask me hard questions about my proposal.” Students rehearse responses.

•  NOT ALLOWED: AI writing the proposal, analysis, or reflection for students.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  HMW sentence starter card

•  Pre-made survey template (students adapt)

•  Prototype with teacher-provided materials

•  Partner project option

•  Simplified proposal form with sentence frames

Extensions

•  Present to district leadership or school board

•  Launch actual solution (fundraiser, app, campaign)

•  Write a full grant proposal for funding

•  Document the process as a short documentary

💡 Reflection Prompts

  • What problem did you choose and why does it matter to you personally?
  • What did your research reveal that surprised you about this problem?
  • How did your prototype change between version 1 and the final version?
  • What would you do differently if you had 2 more weeks?
  • How did AI support your process — and what did you have to do that AI could not do?

📂 Portfolio Artifacts

  • HMW problem statement
  • Survey data + analysis
  • Formal proposal document
  • Prototype photos (v1 + final)
  • Pitch presentation (recording or slide deck)
  • AI log
  • Reflection essay

Project 2: Documentary: A Story That Needs to Be Told

⏱  Time Frame: 5–6 weeks

❓  Driving Question: What truth, story, or issue deserves a spotlight, and how can I use film to share it with the world?

🎯 Learning Goals

  • Research a topic in-depth using primary and secondary sources
  • Conduct at least one original interview to gather firsthand perspective
  • Write, storyboard, film, and edit a short documentary (4–8 minutes)
  • Use the documentary medium to communicate evidence-based arguments or stories

📅 Daily Breakdown

Day(s)

Focus

Activities

Days 1–3

Topic & Thesis

• Choose topic: local, national, or personal interest

• Write a thesis/central argument for the documentary

• Research background information (3–5 sources)

Days 4–6

Interview Prep

• Identify 1–2 interview subjects (experts, community members, peers)

• Write 6–8 interview questions

• Practice interviewing a classmate first

Days 7–9

Script & Storyboard

• Write a full script: hook, context, evidence, interview clips, conclusion

• Create a storyboard: scene by scene shot plan

• Plan B-roll footage list

Days 10–13

Film & Edit

• Film narration, interviews, and B-roll

• Edit using iMovie, CapCut, or similar

• Add titles, music, and captions

Days 14–15

Screen & Reflect

• Film premiere: screen for class and/or invited guests

• Q&A with audience

• Reflection essay and portfolio capture

🏫 Scripted Mini-Lesson: Asking Great Interview Questions

Day 5 — 20 minutes

•  SAY: “An interview is only as good as the questions you ask. Weak questions get short answers. Strong questions reveal stories.”

•  SHOW three question types:

•     Closed: “Are you worried about climate change?” (yes/no)

•     Leading: “Don’t you think the government should do more?” (leads the answer)

•     Open + Specific: “Can you describe a moment when you personally felt the effects of climate change?”

•  WHY OPEN + SPECIFIC WORKS: It invites a story, it’s unpredictable, it creates emotional connection.

•  PRACTICE: Students write their 6 questions, label each type, and revise any closed or leading ones.

•  ROLE PLAY: Students interview a partner and flag questions that felt too easy to answer with just ‘yes’ or ‘no.’

•  DEBRIEF: “The best documentaries make you feel something. Great interviews are the heart of that.”

🤖 AI Integration

Approved AI Uses

•  Research support: “Summarize what research says about [topic]. List 3 different perspectives.” Students read and verify each.

•  Script feedback: “Read my documentary hook. Is it engaging? Is anything confusing?” Students revise based on suggestions.

•  B-roll ideas: “What visuals would make a documentary about [topic] more compelling?” Students select and film what fits.

•  Caption writing: “Here is my interview clip. Write a 10-word caption that summarizes the main point.” Students edit for accuracy.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Storyboard template (8 boxes)

•  Pre-made interview question stems

•  Co-interview with a partner

•  Slideshow ‘documentary’ instead of filmed version

•  Shorter format: 2–3 minutes instead of 4–8

Extensions

•  Submit to a student film festival

•  Add original music or score

•  Translate with subtitles for a bilingual audience

•  Create a companion written article for the school newspaper

📂 Portfolio Artifacts

  • Research notes + bibliography
  • Script + storyboard
  • Interview questions + recording
  • Final documentary file
  • AI log
  • Reflection essay

Project 3: The Ultimate How-To: Design a Digital Guide

⏱  Time Frame: 3–4 weeks

❓  Driving Question: What skill, hobby, or process do I know well enough to teach to someone else through a polished digital guide?

🎯 Learning Goals

  • Identify a skill or process they have genuine expertise in
  • Organize procedural information into logical, clear steps
  • Design a digital guide using media, visuals, and professional layout
  • Test the guide with a real user and revise based on their experience

📅 Daily Breakdown

Day(s)

Focus

Activities

Days 1–2

Topic & Audience

• List 5 things they know how to do well

• Choose one that others genuinely want to learn

• Define audience: age, prior knowledge, purpose

Days 3–4

Outline & Research

• List all steps needed (detailed task analysis)

• Research to fill any gaps in knowledge

• Add tips, warnings, and common mistakes

Days 5–8

Create the Guide

• Design using Google Slides, Canva, a website, or video format

• Add photos, diagrams, or screen recordings for each step

• Apply visual design principles: contrast, hierarchy, white space

Days 9–10

User Test & Revise

• A person outside class tries to follow the guide

• Collect specific feedback: What was confusing? What was missing?

• Revise guide based on testing data

Days 11–12

Publish & Present

• Publish guide on class website, Google Site, or printed booklet

• Present overview to class: what, who, why

• Reflection

🤖 AI Integration

Approved AI Uses

•  Audience analysis: “My guide teaches [topic] to [audience]. What are 3 things this audience probably already knows vs. 3 things I’ll need to explain from scratch?”

•  Writing clarity: “Here is Step 4 of my guide. Is this clear? What might confuse a 6th grader?” Students revise accordingly.

•  Visuals: “Describe an image I could create or find to illustrate Step 2 of [process].” Students design or source the visual.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Topic list of common student skills (cooking, gaming, sports, art, coding)

•  Step-by-step outline template

•  Simplified format: 5-step poster instead of digital guide

•  Partner project with divided steps

Extensions

•  Create a video tutorial version

•  Build a website with multiple how-to pages

•  Teach the skill live to a younger class

•  Add a troubleshooting FAQ section

📂 Portfolio Artifacts

  • Final digital guide (link or file)
  • User testing notes + feedback
  • Revision log (what changed and why)
  • AI log
  • Reflection

Project 4: Historical Fiction Worldbuilding

⏱  Time Frame: 5–6 weeks

❓  Driving Question: How can I use accurate historical research to build a fictional world and tell a story that could have actually happened?

🎯 Learning Goals

  • Conduct accurate research on a historical time period, place, or event
  • Apply historical details authentically to fictional characters and settings
  • Write a multi-chapter short story (8–15 pages) with a clear plot arc
  • Distinguish between historical fact and creative invention in their writing

📅 Daily Breakdown

Day(s)

Focus

Activities

Days 1–3

Choose Era & Research

• Select a time period / historical event of passion

• Research: social life, clothing, language, food, major events, conflicts

• Build a “World Bible”: factual details they will use in the story

Days 4–6

Character & Plot Design

• Create 2–3 original characters with detailed profiles

• Plot outline: beginning, rising action, climax, resolution

• Map the setting: describe the physical environment

Days 7–11

Write First Draft

• Write one chapter per day (600–1000 words each)

• Weekly mini-lessons: dialogue, historical detail integration, pacing

• Peer writing partners give chapter-by-chapter feedback

Days 12–14

Revise & Polish

• Read aloud draft: catch awkward phrasing

• Check for historical accuracy: does every detail hold up?

• Final edit for grammar, dialogue punctuation, and consistency

Days 15–16

Publish & Present

• Bind or publish digitally

• Author’s talk: explain research process and creative choices

• Include a “Author’s Note” separating fact from fiction

🏫 Scripted Mini-Lesson: Weaving Research Into Fiction

Week 2 — 20 minutes

•  SAY: “The best historical fiction makes you FORGET it’s fiction. The author has done so much research that the world feels completely real.”

•  READ two paragraphs aloud — one with bare description, one rich with historical detail (teacher-created examples).

•  DISCUSS: What specific details made the second one feel real? (food names, tools, clothing terms, social customs)

•  TEACH: The “Hide the Research” technique: Never say ‘In 1863 people wore...’ Instead, show a character wearing the actual thing.

•  DO: Students take one scene from their draft and add 3 historically accurate sensory details.

•  SHARE: Partners read each other’s revised scenes and identify the details that felt most authentic.

•  DEBRIEF: “Your research is invisible when it’s done well — but the reader FEELS it everywhere.”

🤖 AI Integration

Approved AI Uses

•  World building: “What would a typical morning look like for a 12-year-old living in [city] in [year]?” Students verify details against research sources before using them.

•  Dialogue: “Does this dialogue sound historically plausible for [time period]? What words or phrases would feel anachronistic?”

•  Plot feedback: “Read my plot outline. Does the conflict feel strong enough? What could create more tension?”

•  NOT ALLOWED: AI writing chapters, generating character voices, or creating the story plot.

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  World Bible template with labeled categories

•  Character profile sheet with sentence starters

•  Shorter format: 3–5 chapter story

•  Pre-selected time periods with curated research sources

Extensions

•  Write an Author’s Note citing all historical sources used

•  Submit to a young writers competition

•  Create illustrations for each chapter

•  Research and write a comparative essay: fact vs. fiction in the story

📂 Portfolio Artifacts

  • World Bible (research notes)
  • Character profiles
  • Plot outline
  • Final story (bound or digital)
  • Author’s Note
  • AI log
  • Reflection

Project 5: Community Advocacy Campaign

⏱  Time Frame: 5–6 weeks

❓  Driving Question: What issue in our community do I care about enough to take real action on, and how can I design a campaign that creates change?

🎯 Learning Goals

  • Research a real community issue using primary and secondary data
  • Identify a specific audience and design persuasive communication for them
  • Create and execute a multi-piece campaign (poster, video, social media content, event)
  • Measure the campaign’s actual impact through data and testimonials

📅 Daily Breakdown

Day(s)

Focus

Activities

Days 1–2

Issue Selection

• Brainstorm issues they care about: school, local, national, environmental, social

• Research the issue from 2+ perspectives

• Write a compelling problem statement

Days 3–5

Audience & Message

• Define target audience: who needs to act?

• Craft a central message in one sentence

• Research effective advocacy campaigns as models

Days 6–9

Create Campaign Materials

• Design 3+ materials: poster, video, social post, flyer, petition, event plan

• Apply persuasion techniques: logos, ethos, pathos

• Peer critique of materials

Days 10–12

Launch & Measure

• Launch campaign within school or community

• Collect data: signatures, survey responses, views, attendance

• Document reactions and impact

Days 13–15

Report & Reflect

• Create an impact report: what changed? how many people were reached?

• Present campaign to class or community

• Reflection on advocacy, passion, and civic engagement

🤖 AI Integration

Approved AI Uses

•  Research: “Summarize 3 different perspectives on [issue] and note where they agree and disagree.” Students evaluate and check against sources.

•  Messaging: “Here is my campaign message. Is it clear? Does it feel urgent? What might turn off my target audience?”

•  Social post drafts: “Write 3 different versions of an Instagram caption for my campaign. Use different emotional tones.” Students select and revise one.

•  Impact framing: “How might I present my campaign data in a compelling way?”

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Issue menu with pre-researched background on 10 topics

•  Campaign planning template

•  Fewer required materials (2 instead of 3+)

•  Partner campaign option

Extensions

•  Present campaign to city council or school board

•  Partner with a local nonprofit

•  Track media coverage of the issue over one month

•  Submit advocacy piece to local newspaper

📂 Portfolio Artifacts

  • Problem statement + research notes
  • Campaign materials (photos/files)
  • Impact data report
  • Presentation recording or slides
  • AI log
  • Reflection essay: What did I learn about advocacy and my own values?

Project 6: Game Design Challenge: Build a Learning Game

⏱  Time Frame: 4–5 weeks

❓  Driving Question: How can I use what I know about a subject I love to design a game that teaches other people?

🎯 Learning Goals

  • Apply research and content knowledge to a creative design project
  • Understand game mechanics and how they create engagement
  • Iterate through multiple playtest rounds with real user feedback
  • Balance entertainment and learning in a designed product

📅 Daily Breakdown

Day(s)

Focus

Activities

Days 1–2

Game Exploration & Topic

• Analyze 2–3 existing educational games: what makes them work?

• Choose a topic they know well that others could learn

• Research the content needed for the game

Days 3–4

Design the Game

• Define: game type (board game, card game, quiz game, digital)

• Write the rules and learning objectives

• Design game board/cards/digital layout

Days 5–8

Build & Playtest

• Build prototype version 1

• Internal playtest: does it work? Is it fun? Do people learn?

• Revise and build version 2

Days 9–10

External Playtest

• Another class or group playtests the game

• Collect structured feedback forms

• Final revisions

Days 11–12

Game Launch

• Official “game day” presentation and play session

• Reflection: What did players actually learn?

• Portfolio capture

♿ Differentiation

Scaffolds

•  Game mechanics reference sheet (5 common game types)

•  Pre-made game board template

•  Partner design team

•  Simplified rules format: 5 bullet points max

Extensions

•  Develop a digital version using Scratch or Google Slides

•  Design expansion pack with advanced levels

•  Write a game design document (professional format)

•  Submit to a school game festival or maker fair

📂 Portfolio Artifacts

  • Game design document (rules, learning goals, mechanics)
  • Photos of prototype versions
  • Playtest feedback forms
  • Final game (photo or file)
  • Reflection: Did players learn? How do you know?
